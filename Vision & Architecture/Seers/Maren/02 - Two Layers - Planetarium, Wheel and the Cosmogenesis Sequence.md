**The Distinction:**

Forrest makes this precise: there are *two solar systems*. One is for astronomers — the
objective arrangement of planets orbiting the sun, viewable from a billion miles above.
The other is for astrologers — the system as it relates to *us*, standing on Earth,
watching the sky from a specific place at a specific moment.

The houses belong entirely to the second system. They are not out there in space. They
are **sections of sky relative to where you stand**. To say a planet is in the First House
is to say it was just beneath the eastern horizon *at the moment of your birth, from the
place of your birth*. Move a few miles and the house positions change. The houses are
completely dependent on point of view.

The planets, however, *are* out there. They move through space. They occupy positions
in the zodiac that are the same for everyone born on the same day, anywhere on Earth.
What changes is *which house* those planets fall into — because the houses are the local
frame, the slice of sky above *your* ground.

**So how do you build this?**

You need **two layers of architecture**, and the person must understand they are
moving between them.

**Layer One: The Planetarium — Cosmological Space**

This is where you begin. The person floats in the dark, surrounded by the planets as
luminous bodies. This is the astronomer's solar system made navigable — not literally to
scale, but *archetypal*. Mercury close to the center, small and quick. Jupiter immense.
Saturn ringed and distant. Pluto at the edge, barely visible, pulling.

In this space, the planets are **themselves**. Not yet in relationship to the person. The

*raw energies* before they become personal.

But this space is **pre-birth**. It is the cosmos before the soul has descended into a
specific point of view. It is vast, impersonal, beautiful — and not yet *theirs*.

**The Threshold: Birth**

When the person enters their birth data, something shifts. The cosmological space
*rotates*. The planets do not move — but the frame changes. Suddenly there is a
horizon line. There is an eastern point and a western point. There is *above* and
*below*. The person has landed.

This is the moment the houses come into being. Arroyo says the houses are fields of
experience — they emerge the instant there is a *where* and a *when*. Before birth,
there are only planets in signs. After birth, there is a chart: planets in signs *in houses*.

**Layer Two: The Wheel — Experiential Space**

Now the person stands at the center of a different architecture. Not floating among
planets, but standing on ground, with the wheel of houses around them like chambers of
a great circular temple. The horizon line cuts through — the Ascendant to the east, the
Descendant to the west. Above is the visible sky. Below is the invisible.

The planets they saw in the planetarium are now *placed*. Mars, which they approached
as pure energy in the cosmological space, is now *in a house* — their Third House,
perhaps, or their Tenth. It has a location in their life. It has a territory it governs, a field
of experience it energizes.

**The Navigable Journey:**

1. **The person begins in the Planetarium.** They orient to the planets as cosmic

Jupiter in the Fourth House, then when they descend into the well beneath the house,

into the cosmological view — to see the whole system, to remember that the planets are
not just personal but *cosmic*. This toggle between views teaches something: you are
both a local self and a participant in something vast.

The solar system formed about 4.6 billion years ago through a process called nebular
collapse. Here's how it unfolded:
The Trigger
A giant cloud of gas and dust (a nebula) began to collapse under its own gravity —
possibly triggered by a nearby supernova shockwave. As it collapsed, it began to spin
and flatten into a rotating disk called the solar nebula.
The Sun Forms
Most of the material (over 99%) fell toward the center, where pressure and

temperature became so extreme that hydrogen nuclei began fusing into helium. The
Sun ignited.
Planets Coalesce
In the remaining disk, particles collided and stuck together in a process called
accretion:

Dust grains clumped into pebbles → boulders → planetesimals (kilometer-scale bodies)
Planetesimals collided to form protoplanets
Over tens of millions of years, protoplanets swept up surrounding material and
became the planets we have today

Why Inner vs. Outer Planets Differ
The early Sun blasted out intense radiation and solar wind. This pushed lighter
volatile gases (hydrogen, helium, water, methane) outward past the frost line — the
boundary beyond which ices could survive. That's why:

Inner planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) are small and rocky — only heavy
materials could survive close in
Outer planets (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune) are gas and ice giants — they had
access to far more material

The Late Heavy Bombardment
After the main formation period, gravitational interactions — especially Jupiter's
massive pull — sent debris raining across the inner solar system in a period called the
Late Heavy Bombardment (~4 billion years ago), cratering the rocky planets and
Moon heavily.
What's Left Over

prevented from forming a planet. The Kuiper Belt and Oort Cloud are icy remnants
